Montréal Crime Forecast Study

Forecasting study · Dora Zhuang

When can monthly crime forecasts be trusted?

I tested forecasts of recorded incidents in Montréal at 77 different starting points between 2019 and 2026. Models beat "same month last year" by about 19% overall. They failed, along with their uncertainty ranges, when vehicle theft tripled.

Uncertainty ranges fail when they matter

An 80% interval should contain the outcome 80% of the time. Across all categories it contained 50% in 2020 and 61% in 2021, then recovered once the shocks had passed.

Recalibrating with past errors overcorrects to 91%, because the calibration history still holds the shock. A version that adapts online stays at 81%.

Share of outcomes inside the ETS 80% interval, by year of the forecast month. Dashed line marks the 80% target.

For small districts, simple pooling beats separate models

Split across 29 police districts, a typical series has 14 incidents a month. Dividing the citywide forecast by each district's share of the last year is more accurate than a model per district. One model trained on all 145 district series scores 1% better than that split, a gap too small to tell from chance.

MASE: forecast error divided by the error of "same month last year" in the training data. Below 1 beats that benchmark. Blue marks the best in each column.

How it was tested

  • 365,404 incidents recorded by the SPVM, January 2015 to October 2026.
  • At each of 77 starting points, every model is refit and forecasts 1 to 6 months ahead.
  • ETS, ARIMA and Theta; a pooled Poisson boosting model; two naive benchmarks.
  • No settings were tuned on the test results.

What this does not show

  • These are incidents recorded by police. They reflect reporting and recording practice, and they do not measure neighbourhood risk.
  • The SPVM notes this dataset is not its official statistics, and recent months can be revised.
  • This is an accuracy study. It is not a tool for deploying police resources.
