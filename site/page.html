Forecasting study · Dora Zhuang

When can monthly crime forecasts be trusted?

Between 2020 and 2023, vehicle thefts in Montréal more than doubled. None of the seven methods tested saw it coming, and the uncertainty ranges built to flag that kind of risk missed it too. This study replays six years of forecasts to show where they hold up and where they break.

Uncertainty ranges fail when they matter

An 80% interval should contain the outcome 80% of the time. It contained 50% in 2020 and 61% in 2021, then recovered once the shocks had passed.

The recalibration methods tested did not help. The standard method overcorrects to 91%, and the adaptive one swings from 92% in 2022 to 73% in 2026. The model's own interval scored better in every year.

For small districts, pooling beats separate models

Across 29 police districts, a typical series has 14 incidents a month. Dividing the citywide forecast by each district's share of the last year beats a model per district by 5%. One model trained on all 145 district series shows no clear gain over that split. Pooling is what helps: with the model held fixed, training on all series at once cuts error by 7% against training each alone.

MASE: forecast error divided by the error of "same month last year" in the training data. Below 1 beats that benchmark. Blue marks the best in each column.

Would a shorter memory have helped?

A model that only sees recent data should adapt faster. Refitting on the last two or three years made no clear difference during the surge (error ratio 0.98, interval 0.95 to 1.05) and raised errors on the other four categories by 25% to 45%.

What a planner would have seen

Planning to the forecast's 90th percentile should leave a shortfall one month in ten. It left one in six: 28% of months in 2021, 9% in 2024. That plan still costs 29% less than "last year plus 20%".

How it was tested

  • 365,404 incidents recorded by the SPVM, January 2015 to October 2026.
  • At each of 77 starting points, every model is refit and forecasts 1 to 6 months ahead.
  • ETS, ARIMA, Theta and their average; a pooled Poisson boosting model; two naive benchmarks.
  • Counts are incidents recorded by police, so they reflect reporting practice as well as crime.

Which results hold up

  • Models beat "same month last year": error ratio 0.81, 95% interval 0.71 to 0.86.
  • The gain fades with lead time: 0.60 one month ahead, 0.92 six months ahead, and no clear gain at six months since 2023.
  • No clear difference between ETS and ARIMA (1.00, interval 0.95 to 1.09). Which model matters less than how far ahead and how stable the series is.
